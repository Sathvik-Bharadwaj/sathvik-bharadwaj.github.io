Our thesis

Analog design. An open frontier.

Digital design became software. Analog design never made that transition, and it is still where silicon meets the real world. This is what we believe, and why we are building circuitEvolve.

01 · THE WORLD

The physical world is analog.

Every signal that matters to people starts as something continuous: a voice, a heartbeat, a radio wave, the charge in a battery.

Before a processor can do anything with it, an analog circuit has to sense it, amplify it, filter it and convert it into numbers. On the way back out, analog circuits turn numbers into sound, motion, light and power. Sensors, radios, power management, data converters, clocks and the links that carry data between chips all depend on them.

Analog behaviour is continuous and coupled. A transistor's width and length, the current through it and the capacitor beside it all shape gain, speed, noise, stability and power at the same time. Change one, and the others move.

02 · THE GAP

Digital became software. Analog never did.

Over the past few decades, digital chip design was transformed by automation.

Engineers describe logic in code. Synthesis, placement, routing and verification tools turn that description into billions of transistors, searching enormous design spaces on the engineer's behalf. Productivity grew with computation.

Analog design took a different path. Its circuits have far fewer transistors but are far less forgiving: each one is a carefully tuned balance of physics. The core loop is still largely manual. An expert adjusts a value, runs a simulation, reads the waveform, judges the result, and goes around again.

The tools simulate. The engineer still searches.

03 · THE COST

The loop that eats the schedule.

A single amplifier has a dozen sizes, bias currents and compensation values, and they all interact.

Every one of them must work not once but across manufacturing variation, temperature and supply voltage: the corners. A change that fixes stability at one corner can break bandwidth at another. So the loop repeats: tweak, simulate every corner, compare, tweak again.

That repetition consumes the time of the scarcest people in semiconductors, experienced analog designers. Time spent nudging values is time not spent on architecture, on new topologies, or on the questions only an expert can answer.

04 · THE SPACE

The space is larger than intuition.

Ten parameters, each with a wide range of sensible values, already describe more circuits than anyone could simulate one by one.

Most of them fail at least one requirement. A small region meets every target, and inside it lies a front of best trade-offs: designs where you cannot gain more speed without spending more power or giving up gain.

Finding that front by hand means relying on experience and a few well-chosen experiments. It works, and it is how great circuits have always been made. But it leaves most of the space unexplored.

05 · THE SEARCH

Computation can do the searching.

OPTIM treats design as a search, and runs it in your own simulator.

It starts from a design you have already simulated. A model proposes a coordinated change; the circuit is simulated at every selected corner and scored against your targets. The best candidates seed the next generation, and population by population the search moves toward designs that pass everywhere.

No pre-training on proprietary data is needed. The evidence comes from simulating your circuit, with your models, under your conditions.

06 · THE ENGINEER

Engineers stay in control.

Automation is only useful if you can trust it.

You decide what good looks like: the targets, their priorities, which values may change and which conditions must pass. Every candidate is kept with its lineage, its measured results at every corner, the exact netlist changes, the model's written rationale and its cost.

Nothing touches your schematic until you choose a result and publish it.

A higher score is not a pass. The measurements decide.

07 · WHY US

That is why circuitEvolve exists.

We are building toward an analog workflow where designers explore more, understand trade-offs sooner, and move from specification to silicon with confidence.

OPTIM is the first step: simulation-driven optimization for circuits you already have. Our research on NetGen, presented at MLCAD 2026, looks further ahead, at agents that generate analog netlists and learn from their failures.

The goal is the same throughout: give analog engineers the leverage digital engineers have had for decades.
